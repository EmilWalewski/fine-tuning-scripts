Dom Development S.A. Capital Group 2025 Management Report, consolidated sustainability reporting section (pages 103-104) covering climate risk analysis and double-materiality assessment. Climate risk methodology based on IOŚ-PIB Klimada 2.0 climate-change scenarios for Poland in the 21st century, RCP 8.5 scenario benchmarked against the 2011-2020 decade (assuming continuation of current GHG-emission growth pace), plus Urban Adaptation Plans (source klimada2.ios.gov.pl, accessed 01.02.2024). Analysis identified actual and potential pollution sources (dust, noise, chemical emissions) and soil/water impacts; assessed compliance with applicable environmental regulations; and reviewed upstream value chain, i.e. material producers and the sourcing/production of construction materials that may generate pollution. Buildings in dense urban development are on average exposed to climate risks due to the urban heat-island effect and lengthening drought and high-temperature periods; during operation (outside Group control) buildings face thermal stress affecting HVAC, reduced thermal comfort and higher cooling costs. Identified climate risks also affect the construction process and supply chain; extreme rainfall and heat cause project-execution downtime and higher insurance costs. Group did not include systemic biodiversity risks. Group activities and assets are not aligned with EU Taxonomy delegated-act requirements and require significant financial outlays in coming years to achieve climate-neutral economy compliance. Group qualitatively assessed asset exposure to transition climate risks, concentrated on rising technological-transition costs and rising electricity/heat prices, potentially increasing operating expenses (higher land-acquisition and material costs). Short- and medium-term transition events linked to new climate-environmental regulations are tabulated. Group did not perform a detailed financial-sensitivity assessment with long-horizon CAPEX estimates, nor a deepened upstream value-chain analysis, so impacts from primary raw-material extraction remain unknown; no consultation with local communities was conducted at that stage. Biodiversity and ecosystems: Group reviewed resources, operational processes and value chain to determine impacts, risks and opportunities; analysis within operating activity verified legal compliance via review of administrative environmental decisions on biodiversity protection, including environmental impact assessment (OOŚ) requirements and Natura 2000 protection plans. In 2025 the Group executed 6 investments located within 500 m of legally protected nature areas regarded as biodiversity-sensitive (detailed actions in table on pp. 81-83). Local-community consultation via surveys on biodiversity impact yielded statistically insignificant responses with no clear positive/negative impact. Group did not use biodiversity scenario analyses, did not identify/assess its dependency on ecological diversity, ecosystems and related services in own locations and value chain. Group identified and assessed biodiversity-related risks as material (table pp. 100-102) but did not identify physical or transition risks for biodiversity. Water and marine resources: Group reviewed assets, resources, operational processes and value chain to determine related impacts, risks and opportunities, covering own operations (water consumption in construction processes, office-premises operation, potential water-saving actions) and downstream value chain (potential benefits of water-saving solutions). No material financial risks identified; community survey responses statistically insignificant. Resource use and circular economy: Group reviewed resources, operational processes and value chain, covering own operations (Group companies' waste management), downstream value chain (serviced-apartment clients), and upstream value chain (cooperation with waste recipients). Local communities indicated a positive actual impact from business digitalization and process optimizations. Business conduct: Group reviewed resources to determine impacts, risks and opportunities, considering locations (four markets where the Group operates) and business sectors (NACE M.68.11 buying and selling of own real estate; F.41.00 construction of residential and non-residential buildings; L.66.19 other activities auxiliary to financial services except insurance and pension funding; M.68.20 rental and operating of own or leased real estate); existing processes, policies, procedures and legal requirements were reviewed. In the double-materiality process, business-practice impact (corporate culture, whistleblower-protection policies, supplier-relationship management) was deemed material by management and key contractors; community surveys on governance matters showed a positive actual impact via absence of corruption threats during investment execution. Materiality of impact assessed by: for identified actual negative impacts - scale, scope, irreversible character; for potential negative and potential positive impacts - scale, scope, irreversible character, probability of occurrence; for actual positive impacts - scale and scope. Phase 3 prioritized the most material impacts, risks and opportunities identified earlier; impacts deemed material were those whose materiality was important, significant and critical.
